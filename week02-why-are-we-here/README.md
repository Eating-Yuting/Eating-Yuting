# Why Are We Here?

*A reflection on why a designer is learning to program — SD5913, Week 1.*

---

Week 1 of this course, I made a grid of squares fall apart — and then I
made it fall apart into a pink heart. The original is *Schotter*, a
generative artwork by Georg Nees from 1968. I drew my version with a few
lines of Python. It was fun. But fun is not the real answer to the question
this week keeps asking: *why are we here?* Why should designers, in an age
when machines can write code, sit down and learn to program themselves?

Here is my honest list of reasons.

**We are here to learn a new skill.** Not to become engineers, but to
add one more tool next to paper, ink, and layout — a tool that can think
in sequences and rules.

**We are here to raise the level of our work.** A designer who
understands code can design things that a static mock-up can never show:
motion, interaction, response, surprise.

**We are here to make our ideas actually land.** A beautiful concept that
stays in a rendering is only a picture. Code lets a design walk out of the
portfolio and into the real world, where people can touch it.

**We are here to collaborate across fields.** Real projects are built by
designers, developers, and engineers talking to each other. If I can read
even a little of their language, the wall between us gets much lower.

**And we are here to explore the space between art and programming.**
This last one I did not truly believe until this week, when I watched
Dylan Beattie's talk, *The Art of Code*.

Three moments from that film stayed with me.

First, music. When Beattie turned code into a rock song — and the code
itself *was* the lyrics — I sat up. I always thought code and music lived
in different worlds, but there they were, the same object, read two ways,
and both of them beautiful.

Second, a small, silly bird game. Someone built a whole playable game out
of a modest few lines of code, and it was adorable. It reminded me that
programming is not always giant systems in dark server rooms; it can be a
tiny, charming toy that makes people smile.

Third, the patterns that grow and arrange themselves like living things.
Rules as simple as a sentence, repeated over and over, produced structures
that looked biological — alive. That is exactly the kind of order-born-from-chaos
I had just met in *Schotter*, and suddenly the two experiences
connected: my falling-apart squares and these self-growing patterns are
the same idea, one drawn by hand, one grown by rules.

My own week told me the same story from a harder angle.

My first push to GitHub was rejected with "403: permission denied." I was
a visitor in the course repository — read access, no writing. My own
repository accepted the same push immediately. Code, I learned, is not
magic. It is a set of agreements, and once you understand the grammar you
can read why things fail.

Later, a single garbled character broke my program. No tool could tell me
what I *wanted* it to do; I had to find the line and fix it myself. In an
age when AI can generate code for anyone, this is exactly why we are here.
The more code machines write, the more valuable it is to be the person who
can *read, judge, and direct* it. Literacy, not memorisation, keeps the
authorship in the designer's hands.

So, why are we here? To gain a skill, to lift our work, to make ideas
real, to collaborate, and to explore the borderland between art and code —
and, maybe most of all, because a grid of squares that falls apart into a
heart is proof that logic can carry feeling. I want to be a designer who
can work on both sides of that border.

*— Eating-Yuting, September 2026*
