# Why Are We Here?

*A reflection on why a designer is learning to program — SD5913, Week 1.*

---

If I sum up my first week of this course, it fits in seven lines — the
seven lines I wrote in my notes, in plain English, the way I really think:

> change some numbers
> change ot the heart
> try to make a chocolate games with AI
> git add them
> read the movie
> connect programme with other areas
> met some bug

Here is what those lines actually mean, and why they answer the question
*why are we here*.

**Change some numbers.** I started from *Schotter*, a generative artwork
by Georg Nees from 1968 — a grid of squares, perfect at the top and
falling apart towards the bottom. My teacher said: change the numbers and
run it again. So I did. COLS, ROWS, SEED, CHAOS — each number was a small
decision, and each run gave me a new artwork in one second. I realised I
was not drawing anymore. I was *describing* what I wanted, and the machine
drafted it for me.

**Change of the heart.** Then I wanted the chaos to become a shape. I put
the classic heart equation into the code, and the program skipped every
square outside it. The falling squares formed a pink heart. I did not draw
it — I described it. A "change of heart" in both senses: the grid changed
into a heart, and something in me changed too.

**Try to make a chocolate game with AI.** I pushed further: could the 2D
artwork become a 3D chocolate bar that cracks apart? I built it with AI's
help, and it worked — drag to rotate, a slider to control how fast it
falls to pieces. This is the part AI plays: it can generate code fast.
But *I* decided what the chocolate should feel like, tested it, and knew
what to change. AI drafts; the designer directs.

**Git add them.** Every version I liked, I committed and pushed to
GitHub. At first these commands looked like spells. Now they feel like a
habit of mind: do something, save it, describe it, share it. Version
control is not just a tool — it is a way of keeping your thinking honest.

**Read the movie.** Dylan Beattie's *The Art of Code* gave my week a
soundtrack. Three moments stayed with me: code that *is* a rock song's
lyrics, read two ways and beautiful both times; a tiny, adorable bird
game built from a modest few lines; and patterns that grow and arrange
themselves like living things. Code is not only engineering — it is a
creative medium, with humour and beauty of its own.

**Connect programming with other areas.** This is why I believe we are
here. We learn code to gain a new skill next to paper and ink; to raise
the level of our work beyond static mock-ups; to make ideas actually land
in the real world; to collaborate with developers and engineers across
the wall of language; and to explore the borderland between art and
programming — the same borderland where a 1968 plotter drawing and my
pink heart both live.

**Met some bugs.** My first push was rejected with "403: permission
denied" — I was a visitor in the course repository, not its owner. Later
a single garbled character broke my program and it refused to run. Each
bug was frustrating, and each one taught me more than a smooth success
would have: code is not magic, it is a set of agreements, and once you
read the grammar you can see why things fail.

So, why are we here? In an age when AI can write code for anyone, the
answer is not "to memorise syntax". We are here to become the people who
can read, judge, and direct — who can describe a feeling precisely enough
that a machine can draw it, and who can stay calm when the machine says
*no*.

Seven lines. One heart. A chocolate bar that breaks. And a designer who
is no longer afraid of the terminal.

*— Eating-Yuting, September 2026*
